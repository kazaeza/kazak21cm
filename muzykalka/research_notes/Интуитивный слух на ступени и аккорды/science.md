Scope note for the report writer: this note was compiled from ~18 web calls. Many sources could be reached only as search snippets or abstracts (several PDFs came back unreadable). Every claim below is tagged with the source it came from; where I only saw a secondary summary I say so. A large number of the specific works named in the assignment (Sloboda, Hoppe, Fujioka, Zatorre, Pfordresher, Elmer, Wong "relative pitch training", Gordon's own research) could NOT be verified in this session and are listed under Gaps rather than asserted. No effect sizes for scale-degree/chord-function ear training were found.

## 1. Scale-degree "qualia", tonal hierarchy, tendency tones: are they real, learnable, context-dependent?

### Takeaway
Listeners (musicians and non-musicians) do share a stable tonal hierarchy (tonic triad > other diatonic > chromatic) that tracks how often degrees occur in real music, which points to implicit statistical learning. Degree "feelings" (qualia) are only moderately consistent and are significantly shaped by the chord sounding under the note (Arthur 2018), so a degree is not a fixed isolated "colour" but a context-dependent percept; the evidence that qualia are learnable by training is indirect (no training study found).

### Cited Findings
- Probe-tone method (Krumhansl & Shepard 1979; Krumhansl & Kessler 1982): listeners rate how well each of the 12 pitch classes fits a tonal context on a 1-7 scale; result is "key profiles" with tonic-triad tones highest, other diatonic tones next, non-diatonic lowest. Marvin notes the major and minor profiles are strikingly similar, with 1̂ and 5̂ highest and ♭3̂ rated higher than 3̂ in minor. — [Marvin, "Rethinking Aural Skills Instruction through Cognitive Research: A Response", MTO](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Krumhansl & Kessler (1982), Psychological Review 89, 334-368: listeners rated probe tones after each chord of 10 chord sequences, 8 of which contained modulations, tracking dynamic changes in perceived key. — [Krumhansl & Kessler 1982 (PDF)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/61509123dbba3159acb3709f/t/615093380580ec0880020ca5/1380667475029/KrumhanslKessler1982TracingPerceviedTonalOrg-SpatialRep-Keys.pdf)
- Krumhansl (2001) reported a strong correlation between listener key-profile ratings and the frequency of scale degrees in Western tonal repertoire (as reported by Marvin; the original was not read). — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Castellano, Bharucha & Krumhansl (1984) showed that listeners absorb scale-degree frequency distributions of unfamiliar music (North Indian) rapidly and without conscious effort; Temperley & Marvin (2008) showed listeners can infer tonic and mode from quasi-random melodies built to match major/minor key-profile distributions (both as reported by Marvin; originals not read). — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Huron, *Sweet Anticipation* (MIT Press, 2006): expectation generation depends on statistical learning; the review states that statistical analysis suggests expectation of these features is learned and not innate; Huron is the standard reference for scale-degree qualia (e.g. the "leading" quality of the leading tone). — [Stewart review of Sweet Anticipation (Goldsmiths repository)](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/7/1/PSY-Stewart2007b_GRO.pdf); qualia attribution via [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Arthur (2018), Music Perception 35(3):295-314: listeners with and without music-theory training rated the qualia of scale degrees after chord progressions each ending on a different final harmony. Result: scale degrees show relatively consistent qualia, but the local chord context significantly influences qualia ratings; profiles correlate overall with Krumhansl & Kessler's, but the K&K claim that tonal stability explains the high ratings of tonic-triad members is better explained by the effect of local chord context; musicians were more consistent than non-musicians (both groups fairly consistent). Exact sample size not retrieved. — [Search-result summary of Arthur 2018 abstract](https://scite.ai/authors/claire-arthur-vdDNVv); related dissertation record: [Arthur 2016, "When the Leading Tone Doesn't Lead: Musical Qualia in Context"](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsndl,edsndl.OhioLink.oai.etd.ohiolink.edu.osu1461254823) (secondary sources; verify against the Music Perception page/DOI before quoting numbers)
- Methodological caveat from a later paper describing Arthur's work as a follow-up to Huron's survey: participants opted out of rating the qualia dimension on 12.3% of trials vs 5% on average for other scales, i.e. qualia are harder to rate than other dimensions. — [arXiv 2103.09709, "Do Picardy thirds smile? Tonal hierarchy and tonal valence"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.09709)
- Karpinski (2021, MTO) frames solmization choice around perception/cognition of listening, building on his 1990 perception model and 2000 *Aural Skills Acquisition*; his argument draws on verbal descriptions and listener ratings of qualia, which Marvin says lack corroborating corpus transitional-probability evidence (e.g. how often 7̂ goes to 1̂). — [Karpinski, "A Cognitive Basis for Choosing a Solmization System", MTO 27.2](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/616/148); [Marvin response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)

### Inferences
- Because Arthur found chord context significantly modulates qualia, training a degree only against a bare tonic/drone likely yields a context-bound feel; practising the same degree over several different chords (the "degree over different chords" design) is the approach consistent with this finding. This is my inference, not tested in a training study.
- Qualia are "relatively consistent" but not strongly agreed, and rating them is hard (12.3% opt-outs), so marketing language such as "every degree has a universal colour" overstates the evidence; "tendencies shaped by key + chord + statistics of the repertoire" is better supported.
- Since profiles mirror corpus frequencies and listeners learn distributions implicitly, extensive exposure to the target style (guitar-pop/rock songs) is the likely route to degree intuitions; this is consistent with, but not proven by, the cited studies.

### Gaps
- Exact sample sizes, effect sizes and stimulus details of Arthur (2018) were not retrieved (only abstract-level secondary summaries).
- No study found that trains scale-degree qualia and measures change in qualia ratings or in degree identification (so "are qualia learnable by training" is unanswered directly).
- Huron's own qualia survey data (Sweet Anticipation, ch. on scale-degree qualia) was not retrieved.
- The "A Theory of Scale-Degree Qualia" title could not be located; check whether the caller meant Arthur 2018 (Music Perception) or the 2022 "Genus/Species Account".

## 2. Training relative pitch in adults: scale-degree vs interval vs chord-function training, duration, transfer

### Takeaway
I found no RCT or meta-analysis that directly compares interval, scale-degree and chord-function training in adults. Available evidence is indirect: long-term music training is linked to better relative-pitch performance; perceptual-learning research suggests transfer is often narrow; absolute-pitch training studies show multi-week training is needed and results vary a lot between individuals; a 2026 paper argues non-AP listeners identify degrees via relative pitch and calls for tailored ear training.

### Cited Findings
- Schellenberg & Moreno (2010): participants with music training outperformed others on tests of pitch-processing speed and relative pitch (correlational/lessons-in-general, not targeted degree or interval training). — [Schellenberg & Moreno 2010 (PDF)](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergMoreno2010.pdf) (seen via search snippet)
- Absolute pitch (AP) can be learned by some adults (PLoS ONE 2019, Van Hedger, Heald & Nusbaum): after an extended (reported as eight-week) training programme, a majority showed only modest improvement and only some adults reached near-AP levels; adults with high auditory working memory did best; gains were reported retained for months. Exact N and statistics not retrieved (abstract page was blocked). — [PMC6759182](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6759182/) (search-snippet summary; PubMed page unreadable)
- A review of AP training in a 2024 paper reports that extensive perceptual training of 12-40 hours has been shown to improve pitch-naming for most adult participants. — [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, s13423-024-02620-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-024-02620-2) (search snippet)
- Shibayama, Shimada & Itoh (2026, Frontiers in Psychology): describes relative pitch as context-dependent computation over intervallic structure that determines which scale degree a tone represents; reports that non-AP participants relied consistently on relative pitch and argues for tailored ear training in education. Full text unreadable; details from search snippet. — [Frontiers in Psychology, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1723224](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1723224/pdf)
- Transfer is often narrow in auditory perceptual learning: Ahissar and colleagues found learning did not transfer from temporal-interval to frequency discrimination (secondary report). A 2022 two-week online interval-identification programme in cochlear-implant users produced only a small training effect (not a relevant population for a hearing guitarist). — [Bissmeyer et al. 2022, Frontiers in Neuroscience](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2022.903924/full); [ISAAR proceedings (Ahissar)](https://proceedings.isaar.eu/index.php/isaarproc/article/download/63/60)
- A 2026 study of adolescents concluded digital ear-training tools work best as complements to teachers, most effective for pitch and interval perception, with teacher-led training still essential for harmonic skills (adolescents, so only suggestive for adults). — [Frontiers search result via same query (full-text not read)](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/126027318.pdf) (low confidence on exact attribution)
- Practice-guide advice (non-research, anecdotal): build interval skills first, then practise holding a key and naming degrees. — [music-theory-practice.com](https://music-theory-practice.com/guides/how-to-identify-intervals-by-ear)

### Inferences
- Because transfer between auditory tasks is often narrow, training should target the exact skill needed (degree-in-key and chord-function recognition in real timbres, i.e. guitar), not only isolated interval drills; this is a reasoned extrapolation, not a tested result.
- The AP-training literature suggests dozens of hours and large individual variation; by analogy, expecting weeks-to-months of regular practice for relative degree hearing is reasonable, but no study I found quantifies this for degrees/chords.

### Gaps
- No RCT/meta-analysis of ear training outcomes found (searches returned cochlear-implant and adolescent studies). Not found/verified: Sloboda; Hoppe (2006-era ear training study); Fujioka et al.; Zatorre; Pfordresher; Elmer; Wong "relative pitch training"; Karpinski's own empirical data; Bongiovanni/Heald/Nusbaum/Van Hedger 2023 results (only the title was seen).
- No study found comparing interval vs scale-degree vs chord-function training time-to-criterion.
- A University of Surrey 8-week AP-training study (12 adult musicians; exact accuracy 13.9% to 31.7% on trained timbre, partial transfer to guitar) surfaced in a search summary but the source URL was not confirmed, so I did not cite it as a finding.

## 3. Implicit/statistical learning of harmony: does exposure build function hearing; can it be accelerated?

### Takeaway
Strong evidence that listeners with no formal training implicitly know Western tonal harmony (harmonic priming: faster/more accurate processing of expected tonic vs less stable subdominant targets), with little or no behavioural difference between musicians and non-musicians. Exposure learns harmony implicitly; evidence on how to accelerate it for adults is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Bigand & Poulin-Charronnat (2006), "Are we 'experienced listeners'? A review of the musical capacities that do not depend on formal musical training," Cognition 100, 100-130: untrained listeners with enough exposure perform at a level similar to trained listeners on tasks such as harmonic priming; in eight-chord piano sequences both musicians and non-musicians responded faster/more accurately when the target was a stable tonic than a less stable subdominant. Full text not read; review-level summary. — [lead.ube.fr PDF](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/000002-are-we-experienced-listeners-a-review-of-the-musical-capacities-that-do-not-depend-on-formal-musical-training.pdf)
- Bigand, Tillmann, Poulin, D'Adamo & Madurell (2001), phoneme-monitoring task: both musicians and non-musicians implicitly learned prevalent harmonic structures; both reacted quicker to sequences heard more than once yet showed no explicit knowledge of the familiar sequences (i.e. exposure-based implicit learning works within a session). — [Search summary citing Bigand et al. 2001, Cognition](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergDS2005.pdf) (secondary; original not read)
- Poulin-Charronnat, Bigand, Madurell & Peeremans (2005): harmonic structure affects phoneme processing in sung material across levels of expertise. — [lead.ube.fr PDF](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/000003-the-effect-of-harmonic-context-on-phoneme-monitoring-in-vocal-music.pdf)
- Harmonic-priming effects rarely differ between musicians and non-musicians behaviourally, though Oechslin et al. (2013) report brain responses to harmonic violations do separate listeners by expertise. — [Oechslin et al. 2013 (EPFL PDF)](https://miplab.epfl.ch/pub/oechslin1301.pdf)
- Corrigall, Tillmann & Schellenberg (2022) examined children's harmonic knowledge (title/topic only seen). — [PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/CorrigallEtAl_2022.pdf)
- Marvin (2021) describes degree perception and transitional probabilities as implicitly learned from exposure rather than conscious tallying. — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Popular-music caveat: tonic inference can be harder in popular repertoires with ambiguous loops (Temperley & de Clercq 2013; Richards 2017; Clendinning 2018, cited by Marvin; originals not read). — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)

### Inferences
- Implicit knowledge of function exists in everyone with years of pop-music exposure; the training problem for an adult guitarist is largely mapping existing implicit expectations to explicit labels (degree numbers/functions) and to production on the guitar, rather than building the hearing from scratch. This is my interpretation of the priming literature (priming tasks measure implicit expectation, not explicit naming).
- Because behavioural priming does not differ much between experts and novices, "implicit hearing" and "naming ability" are separable; ear training mainly adds the latter. Not directly tested in the sources.

### Gaps
- No study found that accelerates harmonic implicit learning in adults with a specific schedule (e.g. repeated exposure + feedback) or that converts priming-based implicit knowledge into explicit function labelling.
- Tillmann's own implicit-learning-of-harmony work (artificial grammars, connectionist models) was not directly retrieved.
- Exposure dose (hours of listening) needed was not quantified in any source found.

## 4. Role of tonal context (cadence/drone priming), key-finding speed

### Takeaway
Listeners establish a tonic early and hold it until another centre "defeats" it; tonic is inferred before mode. I found no source giving a reliable number of notes/seconds needed, so any figure should be treated as unknown here.

### Cited Findings
- Tonic and mode are inferred from scale-degree distributions: Temperley & Marvin (2008) (via Marvin). — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Rare intervals (tritone, semitone) help locate position within the scale (Brown, Butler & Jones 1994; Butler 1989; Browne 1981), but other mechanisms also operate (e.g. the opening of Wagner's *Flying Dutchman*, which lacks them); Butler (1990) and Krumhansl (1990) debated how listeners find the tonic. — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Butler's view (cited by Marvin): listeners establish a tonal centre early and keep it until another centre "defeats it"; Matsunaga & Abe (2012) describe "perceptual inertia." — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Karpinski's "Norwegian Wood" example (2021): listeners can infer the tonic before they can tell major from Mixolydian, which becomes clear only near the end of the opening phrase. — [Marvin MTO response](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Krumhansl & Kessler (1982): probe-tone profiles shift with chord context, and the 'tonal hierarchy' is context-established by a cadence/chord sequence. — [Krumhansl & Kessler 1982 (PDF)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/61509123dbba3159acb3709f/t/615093380580ec0880020ca5/1380667475029/KrumhanslKessler1982TracingPerceviedTonalOrg-SpatialRep-Keys.pdf)
- Arthur (2018): the local chord, not just global key stability, significantly influences degree qualia. — [Search summary of Arthur 2018](https://scite.ai/authors/claire-arthur-vdDNVv)

### Inferences
- A sustained drone or tonic-establishing cadence is a plausible way to fix the tonic before asking for a degree (consistent with the finding that tonic is inferred early and kept). No study found that directly tests a drone as a training aid; this is a design inference.
- Ambiguity in looped pop progressions (Temperley & de Clercq; Richards; Clendinning via Marvin) suggests training materials should state the key explicitly rather than expect inferring it from a 4-chord loop.

### Gaps
- No numeric key-finding latency (notes/seconds/chords) found.
- No direct evidence on drone vs cadence priming as a training scaffold.

## 5. Singing/producing vs recognition only (production effect, sensorimotor coupling, audiation)

### Takeaway
Music-psychology work shows that producing/performing melodies improves later memory and perceptual sensitivity to them relative to listening only, and the general production effect is larger for singing; but I found no study directly testing singing-based vs listening-only training for scale-degree or chord-function identification, and no research verification of Gordon's audiation.

### Cited Findings
- Mathias, Tillmann & Palmer: pianists who learned melodies by performing them later showed larger brain responses to altered pitches and detected alterations more accurately than those who only listened. — [McGill eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/f4752n02z); summary [Cognitive Neuroscience Society](https://www.cogneurosociety.org/?p=8398)
- Memory & Cognition study: pianists recognised learned melodies better after auditory-only or auditory-motor learning than after motor-only learning; auditory imagery ability moderated effects. — [Memory & Cognition s13421-011-0177-x (via search)](https://mijn.bsl.nl/article/10.3758/s13421-011-0177-x)
- Production effect: items produced aloud/sung are remembered better than items read silently, and singing showed an even larger advantage in one line of work ("Mechanisms Underlying the Production Effect for Singing"). — [catalogue record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2270011326)
- Singing accuracy relates to vocal-motor control of pitch ("Singing ability is rooted in vocal-motor control of pitch", 2014) (as summarised by a blog; verify at the primary paper). — [PubMed 24621528](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24621528); [blog summary (secondary)](https://fifths-marketing.pages.dev/blog/singing-ear-training-sensorimotor/)

### Inferences
- Having the learner sing or play the degree/chord after hearing it (e.g. sing the degree over a drone, find it on guitar) is consistent with the performance-benefit and production-effect findings, but those studies used melodies/words, not degree-identification, so the benefit for degree hearing is an extrapolation.
- Auditory-only and auditory-motor learning both beat motor-only: pure "finger memory" without listening is not enough; training should keep the ear in the loop.

### Gaps
- No empirical test of Gordon's audiation or of Music Learning Theory found; claims about audiation are theory/pedagogy, not verified here.
- No controlled study of singing vs recognition-only in scale-degree/chord training found.

## 6. Fixed-do vs movable-do vs numbers vs letters; Kodaly/solfege evidence

### Takeaway
Evidence for movable-do/scale-degree systems over fixed-do or letters is mostly theoretical and pedagogical; direct controlled comparisons are sparse. Karpinski and Marvin argue movable do (do-based minor) matches how tonic and degrees are perceived.

### Cited Findings
- Karpinski (2021) favours "do-based minor" (do always = tonic/final regardless of collection); Marvin (2021) supports it citing key-profile experiments and corpus work (profiles of major and minor are similar, 1̂ and 5̂ highest; la-based minor requires hearing the full collection to identify the mode). — [Karpinski MTO](https://www.mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/616/148); [Marvin MTO](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Karpinski's solmization-for-dictation approach (2000) requires finding do quickly and accurately, then assigning solfege. — [Marvin MTO](https://mtosmt.org/ojs/index.php/mto/article/view/712/151)
- Usage vs efficacy: a 1990 survey of U.S. programmes found 57% use some movable system (numbers or movable-do) and only 19% fixed; a literature review found Smith's study concluding that amateur singers improve more with movable than fixed syllables, and that other empirical studies could not be found (search summary; attribution to specific documents is approximate). — [journals.ou.edu (J. Music Theory Pedagogy)](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/607/1438/1429)
- The reference list of Karpinski (2021) includes empirical work on solmization: Smith (1987-1994), Houlahan & Tacka (1992, 1994), Matsunaga & Abe (2005-2012), Lorek et al. (1991), Lorek & Pembrook (2002), Brown, Butler & Jones (1994), Yoshino & Abe (2004), Reifinger (2012), Arthur (2018), Hansberry (2017) (titles/results not read; names from PDF link anchors only). — [Karpinski PDF](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.karpinski.pdf)
- Commercial-site opinion that head-to-head controlled evidence is thin and mixed — treat as opinion only. — [solfege-pro guide](https://solfege-pro.pages.dev/en/guides/movable-do-vs-fixed-do/)

### Inferences
- For a guitarist wanting key-relative hearing, a movable system (numbers or movable-do) fits the target skill (degree = function relative to tonic); absolute letter names serve a different skill (absolute naming). This follows from the movable-do argument but there's no direct trial confirming numbers vs syllables for adult learning speed.
- Numbers vs solfege syllables: no comparative evidence found; syllables may help singing and distinguishing chromatic alterations, numbers help harmony labelling: speculation.

### Gaps
- No direct controlled comparison of numbers vs movable-do vs fixed-do vs letters in adult learners found; Kodaly-method outcome studies not found in this session.
- Karpinski's empirical citations (Smith, Houlahan & Tacka, Matsunaga & Abe) were not read.

## 7. Learning-science principles applicable to ear training

### Takeaway
General cognitive-science evidence strongly supports spacing, interleaving and retrieval practice, with large delayed-test benefits even when practice feels harder; music-specific evidence (interleaved vs blocked practice) is small but supportive. None of it was tested on ear training specifically in the sources I found.

### Cited Findings
- Spacing: Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), Psychological Bulletin 132(3), 354-380: review of 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments in 184 articles; the optimal gap between sessions increased as the retention interval increased. — [PubMed 16719566](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)
- Interleaving: Taylor & Rohrer (2010), Applied Cognitive Psychology: interleaved practice impaired practice-session performance yet roughly doubled test scores one day later (77% vs 38%; effect size reported as 1.21) in 4th-graders learning maths. — [Taylor & Rohrer 2010 record](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1760/)
- Retrieval practice: Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science 17(3), 249-255: on delayed tests prior testing produced substantially greater retention than restudying, even though restudying raised confidence. — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006 (PDF)](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- Music-specific interleaving: Carter & Grahn (2016), Frontiers in Psychology, advanced clarinet players: interleaved practice may be a more effective strategy than continuous repetition; review notes interleaving can impede performance during training but improves long-term learning. Sample/effect not retrieved. — [PMC4989027](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027); violinist study (Mathias & Goldman 2024) reports contextual interference impairs initial learning but improves retention. — [x-mol listing](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1775546406284832768)
- Practitioner post (not a controlled study) advising spacing, interleaving and self-testing for musicians. — [Learning Scientists guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/3/28-1)

### Inferences
- Mixed (interleaved) degree/chord items with spaced review of past errors and delayed retrieval (answer before hearing the key/hint) match the above evidence; expect lower in-session accuracy (a "desirable difficulty") but better retention. This is extrapolation from non-ear-training domains.
- Daily short sessions with spacing across days is better supported than a single long block (Cepeda 2006); no source on optimal session length for ear training.

### Gaps
- Not found: errorless vs errorful learning evidence, immediate-feedback effects, session length, sleep consolidation specific to auditory/music learning; FSRS/spaced-repetition effect sizes for perceptual skills.
- No spacing/retrieval study on aural skills in particular; Rowland (2014) testing-effect meta-analysis was mentioned in a search result but not retrieved.
