# Practitioner methods of "functional ear training": hearing scale degrees, chord function and keys

Evidence labels used below: **[FETCHED]** = I read the page itself; **[SNIPPET]** = only a search-result summary of the page (not read in full; treat as weaker); **[PRACTITIONER]** = a teacher's or blogger's opinion with no data; **[MARKETING]** = app or course sales page; **[FOLKLORE]** = repeated widely but with no verifiable source.

Overall source-quality note: I found no controlled study showing that any of these methods works better than the others. Almost everything is teacher practice, textbook structure, app design or forum advice. Primary sources I could not read: Benbassat's own site (the guessed URL miles.be/software/fet returned 404), Gordon's *Learning Sequences* (1984), Karpinski's manual text (only publisher blurbs), Rogers (1983) "Beyond Intervals", and two PDFs that the fetch tool returned as binary (Telesco's JMTP article, Ted Greene's 1987 ear-training lesson). ## 1. Core "functional" method: context first, then the note (Benbassat / Berklee / Eastman / Kodály / Gordon / Karpinski)

### Takeaway
All the schools agree on one principle: a note or chord is learned by its role relative to an established tonic (a cadence, a drone or a key context), never as an isolated pitch or an interval from the previous note. They differ in how the response is made (singing back to the tonic, hand signs, labelling by degree number or syllable) and in the order in which degrees are introduced.

### Cited Findings
- Benbassat's Functional Ear Trainer plays a cadence to establish the key, then a single note, and you identify that note within that harmonic context. The source is a third-party summary of the official description and a store listing, not Benbassat's own page (404 on the URL I tried). — [SourceForge listing](https://sourceforge.net/app/functional-ear-trainer/) [SNIPPET/MARKETING]; [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1088761926) [SNIPPET/MARKETING]
- A forum or blog report says it is "very common" to push your score in programs like Functional Ear Trainer to 95–100% and still feel tone-deaf on real-world music. The anecdote is a transfer-of-learning warning. — [SNIPPET, summary of search result for "Functional Ear Trainer"; original page not identified](https://sourceforge.net/app/functional-ear-trainer/) [PRACTITIONER]
- Third-party article "Functional Ear Training" (advancingmusician.com) describes an FET-style routine. The author is not Benbassat, and the "repeat button" language suggests a software trainer. — [Advancing Musician](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-training) [FETCHED, PRACTITIONER]:
  - Learn each note "in the context of a key", starting with the lower tetrachord (degrees 1–4) because fewer notes make each one's "unique feeling" easier to pick out.
  - Move on to the upper tetrachord and then the full octave only at roughly 90–100% accuracy.
  - Characteristic sounds are key-independent. Degree 4 has a "pulling tension" and degree 3 sounds restful.
  - Three alternating drills: (a) "Perfection Drill": free replays, sing the mystery note and resolve it to the tonic, answer only when sure; (b) "Inner Ear Gym": aim for 100% with the repeat button banned, so you must resolve the sound in your head; (c) "Real-Life Speed Test": instant answer, no replays, mistakes are data.
  - Chromatic notes are added one at a time to major-scale exercises, in this order: minor 3rd, minor 7th, minor 6th, tritone (b5), then minor 2nd. A second strategy is drilling pairs: M2 vs m2, M3 vs m3, M6 vs m6, M7 vs m7, tritone vs P4/P5. — [Advancing Musician: 2 strategies for chromatic notes](https://advancingmusician.com/2-strategies-to-master-chromatic-notes-with-functional-ear-training) [SNIPPET for the URL; the order was stated on the fetched main page]
  - "10 minutes a day is all you need." Hold a steady 90–100% for several consecutive days before increasing difficulty.
- "Resolve by singing to the tonic" is a documented practitioner technique: sing from the heard note down to the tonic and count the steps, and that count is the degree. A guide says that if it takes three descending steps to get home, you were probably singing degree 3. — [SNIPPET from search "functional ear training … sing back"; origin pages: stringkick.com/scale-degree-ear-trainer](https://www.stringkick.com/scale-degree-ear-trainer/) [PRACTITIONER]
- Tonedear's practice guide for scale degrees: sing up from the root to the given degree, then down from the degree to the root, and check with "hear ascending/descending to root" buttons. — [Tonedear: how to practice](https://tonedear.com/ear-training/how-to-practice) [FETCHED, PRACTITIONER]
- Wikipedia defines functional pitch recognition as identifying the role of a single pitch relative to an established tonic. Movable-do works because identical functions get identical labels. The weakness is key change ("the chief detriment to the movable-do system") and music with no clear tonic. It cites Rogers (1983) as finding song-based interval associations "quite limited in scope". The article carries a "needs inline citations" notice. — [Wikipedia: Ear training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear_training) [FETCHED, encyclopedic, weakly cited]
- Berklee-style sequence for harmonic hearing: set the key with a cadence (I–vi–ii–V–I or ii–V–I), then sing roots of each chord, add the third, then the seventh, then the fifth (the full 1-3-5-7 arpeggio), then guide tones only (3rds and 7ths). The article credits Berklee's ET 1–4 and Roberta Radley's *Real Easy Ear Training Book*, says each step is "often repeated for weeks", and names no direct Berklee quote, so the attribution is unverified. — [fifths-marketing blog: Singing the changes](https://fifths-marketing.pages.dev/blog/singing-the-changes-berklee/) [FETCHED, secondary, possible content-marketing site]
- Kodály/movable-do order (most common American sequence): so–mi, then la (grade 1), do and re (grade 2) completing the pentatonic, and fa and ti last (grade 5) because half steps are harder for young voices. Revised exercises start with the minor third mi–so and add la, do, re one at a time. Hand signs (Curwen, extended by Kodály) show pitch height. Sequences vary, and Kodály held that each nation should build its own sequence from its own folk music. — [Prodigies summary](https://prodigies.com/what-is-the-kodaly-method-how-can-i-use-it/) and [Wikipedia mirror: Kodály method](https://ipfs.ssi.eecc.de/ipns/en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Kod%C3%A1ly_method) [SNIPPET, secondary]
- Gordon's Music Learning Theory: movable-do solfège with la-based minor, used to find the "resting tone" and identify the tonality. Tonal patterns have 2–5 pitches (normally 3) and are learned by singing the patterns and resting tones. Learning moves from discrimination (imitating patterns) through inference to meaning (syllables or function names such as tonic/dominant). The main source is *Learning Sequences in Music* (GIA, 1984), which I did not read. — [MSU dissertation](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/31100/FULL_TEXT/view), [Wikipedia: Audiation](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Audiation) [SNIPPET, secondary]
- Karpinski's *Manual for Ear Training and Sight Singing*: 79 short chapters, each adding "one new complication such as a new rhythm or chord"; dictation, keyboard and sight-singing activities per chapter; second edition adds error-detection and dictation from real music. A practicum review says Karpinski has students label perceived scale degrees (rather than write staff notation) and introduces melodic dictation only after those exercises. The degree order inside the manual is not on the pages I could read. — [W. W. Norton features page](https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9780393614251-manual-for-ear-training-and-sight-singing-6bffa374-5844-4964-b0f8-61b5c82239ac/features) [FETCHED, publisher], [practicum review via search result](https://www.scribd.com/doc/231064683/Practicum-Outline-Copy) [SNIPPET]
- Robert Kelley's university ear-training guide (professor's page) gives a narrow-to-wide order: first decide whether a short melody ends on tonic or dominant; then dominant vs submediant; then other degrees; the same narrowing applies to cadence types and to ii vs IV. Then melody, bass line, harmony, and last inner voices. Rules of thumb in the bass: degree 1 is almost always tonic, 5 and 7 dominant, 6 predominant, 2 and 4 usually predominant. "Sing every scale degree, pattern and progression you learn", and sing the answer to confirm it (is it do or sol?). — [Kelley: Guide to Ear Training](https://www.robertkelleyphd.com/home/eartrain.htm) [FETCHED, professor's guide]
- Telesco's JMTP article is cited by a search summary as arguing that intervals have no meaning outside a harmony or key. I could not read the PDF text. — [Telesco, JMTP](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/801/1642/1632) [SNIPPET]
- A separate JMTP conference report (M. R. Rogers) is quoted as noting that skill at recognising the quality of single chords "does not necessarily lead to success in hearing chords within a tonal progression". It supports teaching chords in progression and not only in isolation. — [JMTP search result](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/790/1631/1621) [SNIPPET; I could not confirm which article this text is in]
- Cadence-first vs drone-only vs bare scale as ways to set the key: a blog argues cadence is better than a bare scale and proposes a fade-out progression (cadence + scale → drone only → silence). This is an opinion piece. — [fifths-marketing: Cadence first](https://fifths-marketing.pages.dev/blog/cadence-first-scale-second/) [SNIPPET, PRACTITIONER, low authority]
- Not found: Benbassat's "do/ti dictation" claim. I could not verify it from any source, so do not state it as part of the method.

### Inferences
- The strongest cross-school consensus (Benbassat-style apps, Karpinski, Kelley, Gordon, Kodály) is: establish key → one target → answer by degree label/syllable → confirm by singing → replay the tonic. This maps directly to an app loop "cadence/drone, then note, then tap degree, then correction by resolving down to tonic".
- Two answer channels are documented: tap/label and sing. Singing (Kelley, Tonedear, Gordon, Kodály, Advancing Musician) appears in every serious source and is cheap to add via the microphone.
- The "tetrachord first, then full octave, 90–100% gate" progression (Advancing Musician) and the "narrow contrasts" approach (Kelley: tonic vs dominant, then dominant vs submediant) agree on the principle: start with few options and add one at a time on an accuracy gate. Gate numbers are the author's choice, not experimentally derived.
- The transfer warning (high scores but still "tone-deaf" on real music) is anecdotal but plausible; it argues for always mixing drills with real-song items (see section 3 and section 6).

### Gaps
- Benbassat's own site and tutorial text (exact cadence, level list, "do/ti" dictation, tonic resolution by singing) were not retrieved.
- Gordon's primary text, Karpinski's table of contents (actual degree and chord order), Rogers (1983) and the Telesco PDF text were not read.
- No Eastman-specific or Berklee primary syllabus text was found (only the secondary blog above).
- No experimental evidence for functional training vs other methods.

## 2. Chord function: home/away/tension, bass-line first, loops and chord pairs

### Takeaway
Practitioners converge on "bass first, function before quality": find the tonic, then identify the bass note's degree (1/4/5 first), then add quality and extensions; start with I–IV–V and add one chord only after about 90% accuracy for three days. Loops and "chord pairs" (I–IV, I–V) are standard small units, and V→I / IV→I are taught as the first cadence contrast.

### Cited Findings
- Bass-line-first for hearing progressions: "often the bass note alone is enough to identify the whole chord". The Musical U lesson sequence is (1) I–V–I: is the dominant above or below the tonic; (2) I–IV–I the same; (3) unprompted identification of I–V vs I–IV without a hint; (4) real songs ("Authority Song", "Imagine", "Baba O'Riley"), singing the bass line. Early exercises tell you where the chord will appear, later ones remove the cue. It teaches Tonic/Subdominant/Dominant, I/IV/V and Do/Fa/Sol as interchangeable names. The page contains no home/away feeling descriptions. — [Musical U: Hearing I–IV–V](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/hearing-chord-progressions-i-iv-v-in-major-keys/) [FETCHED, MARKETING-adjacent course content]
- Musical U's 3-chord-songs lesson: find the I chord (test it by playing the major scale over the melody), derive IV and V by counting up the scale, then work out the order by ear (not necessarily I–IV–V). Practice song: "Love Me Do". It names vi–IV–I–V as "the most important four-chord progression". No data or studies are cited. — [Musical U: 3-chord songs and the 4-chord trick](https://www.musical-u.com/modules/chord-progressions/3-chord-songs-and-the-4-chord-trick/) [FETCHED, MARKETING]
- Musical U's recognition module trains I, IV, V7 and V separately and introduces V7 before plain V because V7 is easier to pick out. — [Musical U: Chord progression recognition](https://www.musical-u.com/modules/chord-progressions/chord-progression-recognition/) [SNIPPET]
- Hooktheory forum thread (a learner community): user DrCav starts with Hooktheory "Beginner" songs (only I, IV, V, vi) in C, plays along on piano without looking at the chords, covers the chord display with cardboard, and turns up the bass. His self-set target is about 200 attempts, roughly 7 a day for a month, then moves to Intermediate songs (I ii iii IV V vi). He cites an unverified claim (attributed to Andrew Fuller) that about 24 attempts reach roughly 80% proficiency. His chord order, following Beatles harmony: I IV V vi, then ii iii, iv, bVII II, III VI, bVI, then stranger chords. Another user (devaski) learns in fragments: V–I and IV–I first, then ii–V–I and deceptive cadences, then repeat for minor. A third noted that minor-key songs contain VII chords even at the Beginner level, and suggested transposing to the relative major. — [Hooktheory forum thread](https://forum.hooktheory.com/t/ideas-for-developing-the-ability-to-hear-chord-progressions-by-ear/159) [FETCHED, forum anecdote]
- Hooktheory Chord Crush: puzzles from real songs with some chords removed; daily "bite-sized" modules (free tier: 5 puzzles and 1 Rush game per day); ELO-style rating; "Level Up!" mode groups exercises by concept. It recommends checking answers against TheoryTab. — [Hooktheory: ear training tips](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/ear-training-tips/) [FETCHED, MARKETING]
- Tonedear: for chord progressions begin with short progressions using only I, IV, V; add one more chord once accuracy is "above 90% for three days in a row"; then the remaining triads, then sevenths. — [Tonedear: how to practice](https://tonedear.com/ear-training/how-to-practice) [FETCHED]
- Kelley: identify tonic and dominant chords in isolation after a short establishing progression; cadence types: authentic V–I (most conclusive), half (ending on V), deceptive (V→vi), plagal (IV–I, usually confirming an earlier authentic cadence). "A dominant is almost always followed by tonic." Classify chords as tonic, dominant (contains the leading tone), or predominant (everything else). Learn root-motion patterns such as 1–4–5 and 2–5–1 by repeated listening. Use melody/bass writing only to confirm functions. — [Kelley](https://www.robertkelleyphd.com/home/eartrain.htm) [FETCHED]
- Beat Kitchen's ear-training guide: "hum the bass", then find which bass note the others pull toward (likely tonic), listen for cadences, then check whether the song ends on the note you are humming. Cadence descriptions are subjective (perfect cadence "sounds like a period", plagal "like a sigh", deceptive "like a plot twist"). — [Beat Kitchen: Finding tonic, hearing cadences](https://beatkitchen.io/guides/musician-basics/06-ear-training-finding-tonic-hearing-cadences/) [FETCHED, PRACTITIONER/MARKETING]
- Deering Banjos blog: V is very often the second-to-last chord of a song; compare how IV and V each resolve to I; practise I–IV–V–I. — [Deering Banjos](https://www.deeringbanjos.com/blogs/deering-banjos-blog/ear-training-hearing-the-difference-between-the-i-iv-and-v-chord) [SNIPPET]
- Podcast summary: perfect fourth and fifth are the most important intervals to start with, and chord changes can be followed through bass movement. — [Harmonious Blacksmith ep. 20](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2408611/episodes/17655133-ep-20-ear-training-how-to-hear-chord-progressions-using-bass-lines-part-1) [SNIPPET]
- Ted Greene's 1987 lesson "Ear training: hearing chord progressions and their basses" exists as a PDF, but the fetch returned binary data, so I cannot report its contents. — [Ted Greene PDF](https://www.tedgreene.com/images/lessons/other/EarTraining_HearingChordProgressionsAndTheirBasses_1987.pdf) [UNREAD]
- Rick Beato's paid "Beato Ear Training" program includes tonal-progression drills and a "4 chords" permutation trainer, and he says he does relative-pitch drills every morning. This is from a search summary and I did not find a free video with the method laid out. — [Beato Ear Training](https://beatoeartraining.com/pages/upgrade) [SNIPPET, MARKETING]
- Epic Ear Training (Joe Luegers) argues that if you can identify the bass scale degrees, you have your chords. — [Epic Ear Training podcast](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2570350/episodes/19355611-a-chord-progression-marathon) [SNIPPET]
- JustinGuitar: I found no dedicated JustinGuitar lesson on hearing I–IV–V changes (a community thread asks the same question). His "Re-Active Listening" lesson is about soloing, not chord function. — [JustinGuitar community](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/re-active-listening/2483) [SNIPPET; negative finding]

### Inferences
- A directly implementable ladder from these sources: (1) I vs V with the bass only, (2) I vs IV, (3) unprompted I/IV/V root, (4) two-chord loops, (5) add vi, (6) ii, then (7) iii and borrowed chords. All of Tonedear, DrCav/Hooktheory, Musical U and Kelley agree on the first four rungs; the later order is the forum's Beatles-frequency heuristic, not tested.
- "Bass first" is consistently recommended, but one forum commenter finds it hard in practice, and cheap phone speakers lose bass. An app should use a clear bass register in synthesis or let the user mute upper voices (an explicit "bass only / full chord" toggle).
- Hiding the notation (DrCav's cardboard) is the same as the app showing only answer buttons, not the chord on screen.
- "Home / away / tension" as feeling words is common in the descriptive sources but I found no source defining a rigorous exercise from it. It is best treated as a UI hint tied to the cadence tendencies (V pulls to I, IV is a gentle departure).

### Gaps
- No primary source for the claim that ii–V–I and I–vi–IV–V loops are the best drill loops; these appear as habitual examples only.
- Ted Greene PDF content, Berklee primary documents and JustinGuitar's ear-training pages were not read.
- No peer-reviewed comparison of "bass-first" with "top-voice first" or "chord-quality first".

## 3. Song anchors for degrees, intervals and chord moves: use and critique

### Takeaway
Song anchors are a mainstream teaching device and a useful scaffold, but the evidence for them is thin and mostly negative on transfer: a cited 1983 paper reportedly found song-interval associations "quite limited in scope", and the better guides present them only as a stepping stone to direct recognition. Hooktheory (TheoryTab / Chord Crush) is the main data-driven way to attach real songs to chord moves.

### Cited Findings
- Wikipedia describes the practice (associate each interval with the first two notes of a popular song) and cites Rogers (1983) as finding such associations "quite limited in scope". I did not read Rogers. — [Wikipedia: Ear training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear_training) [FETCHED]; same point in [Wikipedia: Interval recognition](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Interval_recognition) [SNIPPET]
- Teaching guides present anchors as a stepping stone ("recall the song, then direct recognition"). They must be familiar to the learner, and ascending and descending versions need separate anchors. Anchor lists vary between sites and some examples are disputed. — [Music Theory Practice: how to identify intervals](https://music-theory-practice.com/guides/how-to-identify-intervals-by-ear), [Musiversal blog](https://musiversal.com/blog/interval-training), [Music Theory Practice: reference songs](https://music-theory-practice.com/reference/interval-reference-songs) [SNIPPET]
- The search found no controlled study comparing anchor-based training with other methods. [negative finding from the "song anchors interval recognition evidence critique" search]
- Hooktheory's data tools: TheoryTab (chords as Roman numerals for songs), Trends (draws on over 40,000 songs, chord probabilities, audio previews of progressions in real songs) and per-key cheat sheets listing the 20 most popular chords and songs in the key. — [Hooktheory: Trends tool](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/trends-tool/), [Hooktheory: free resources](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/free-hooktheory-resources/) [SNIPPET]
- Chord Crush is explicitly song-based: puzzles from real songs, with chords removed. — [Hooktheory: ear training tips](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/ear-training-tips/) [FETCHED]
- Musical U exercises use named songs for I–IV–V ("Authority Song", "Imagine", "Baba O'Riley") and "Love Me Do" for three-chord songs. — [Musical U](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/hearing-chord-progressions-i-iv-v-in-major-keys/), [Musical U 3-chord](https://www.musical-u.com/modules/chord-progressions/3-chord-songs-and-the-4-chord-trick/) [FETCHED]
- I found no source that lists a systematic "song for every scale degree" or "song for each chord change" with a verified first-degree list. The lists I saw are interval-based (first interval of a song), which is a different object from a scale-degree anchor. [negative finding]

### Inferences
- Degree anchors differ from interval anchors: a song starting on degree 5 is anchored to the tonic, not to the preceding note. That matches the functional method, so the app's "anchor songs per degree" is more defensible than the usual interval lists, but I have no source testing it either way.
- Given the cited transfer limit, anchors work best as hints that fade: show on a miss, hide as accuracy rises, and require the answer from the "feeling" and not the song. The sources only support "scaffold, not goal".
- Anchors need familiarity and direction: the user's own listening history is better than a generic list.

### Gaps
- Rogers (1983) was not read, so what exactly was tested is unknown.
- No Hooktheory "Earworms" page was found by search under that name; I cannot confirm that feature.
- No empirical data on song-anchor effectiveness.

## 4. Key-finding, tonic-finding, modulation and borrowed chords

### Takeaway
Tonic-finding methods in the sources are: hum the bass and find where it "wants to land", check the song's ending, hum along with a drone, and sing the tonic before a song's final chord. Borrowed chords (iv, bVII, bVI) are taught by A/B contrast in the same key (F → Fm → C), then in real songs; there is almost no rigorous material on modulation hearing.

### Cited Findings
- Beat Kitchen's steps: hum the bass note of each chord, find the destination bass note, listen for cadences, check whether the song ends on the note you are humming; routine: play a song, sing the tonic before it ends. It treats "Sweet Home Alabama" as genuinely ambiguous in key and advises against forcing one answer. — [Beat Kitchen](https://beatkitchen.io/guides/musician-basics/06-ear-training-finding-tonic-hearing-cadences/) [FETCHED, PRACTITIONER]
- Drone practice: sustain a tonic drone and sing or play scale degrees against it to internalise function and intonation. Another trainer plays a drone only (no cadence), so the learner must generate the key internally. — [Benny Sutton: drone practice](https://bennysutton.com/Glossary/drone-practice), [bananatone: functional pitch](https://bananatone.pages.dev/learn/note-ear-training.html) [SNIPPET, low-authority]
- Kelley: be able to sing the tonic at any point, even when it is absent from the final chord's melody or bass; notice when a melody returns to an earlier pitch. — [Kelley](https://www.robertkelleyphd.com/home/eartrain.htm) [FETCHED]
- Wikipedia: functional recognition fails with no clear tonic, and key changes are the "chief detriment" of movable-do. — [Wikipedia: Ear training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear_training) [FETCHED]
- Borrowed chords (from parallel minor): in C major, Fm (iv), Bb (bVII), Ab (bVI). Drill: play F, then Fm, then back to C to hear the colour change; hear bVII–IV–I as the classic-rock move avoiding the dominant pull; start with a basic major-key progression and swap one chord for its borrowed version; drill a small set (bVI, bVII, iv) in isolation, then in songs. A forum poster said they never "heard" modal interchange, which suggests training the "surprise" effect and not chord naming. — [Beat Kitchen: borrowed chords](https://beatkitchen.io/guides/musician-basics/07-the-four-chord-progression-and-borrowed-chords/), [Skoove: modal interchange](https://skoove.com/blog/modal-interchange), [jazzguitar.be forum](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/theory/70701-question-about-modal-interchange-borrowing.html) [SNIPPET, practitioner level]
- Hooktheory forum order for chords: I IV V vi, then ii iii, then iv, bVII II, III VI, bVI, then others. — [Hooktheory forum](https://forum.hooktheory.com/t/ideas-for-developing-the-ability-to-hear-chord-progressions-by-ear/159) [FETCHED, forum anecdote]
- Chromatic scale-note order from Advancing Musician (m3, m7, m6, b5, m2) is the single-note counterpart of borrowed chords. — [Advancing Musician](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-training) [FETCHED]
- "Home base" as a concept appears as vocabulary ("tonic is home"), but Beat Kitchen's page says it is a concept and not a procedure. — [Beat Kitchen](https://beatkitchen.io/guides/musician-basics/06-ear-training-finding-tonic-hearing-cadences/) [FETCHED]

### Inferences
- A "find the tonic" game can be built from the documented steps: play a progression or loop with no stated key, let the user hum or tap the tonic, then play the tonic drone to confirm.
- Borrowed-chord training is best done as paired A/B swaps (IV vs iv, V vs bVII) in a fixed loop first, which maps to the documented "swap one chord" drill.
- Modulation hearing has no systematic method in the sources I read. It is a plausible later-stage drill (a two-key loop with a pivot) but I cannot attribute it.

### Gaps
- No source on modulation-hearing exercises.
- No evidence that drone-only is better or worse than cadence-first.
- The "bVII–IV–I" claim comes from a blog summary and is not backed by data on frequency.

## 5. Daily practice structure, order of difficulty, plateaus

### Takeaway
Sources agree on short daily sessions (10–20 minutes), progression by accuracy gates (90–100% for several days), and few simultaneous options. The most common degree order is the tonic triad and dominant first and the leading tone last; chord order is I–IV–V, then vi, then ii/iii and borrowed. All numeric thresholds are practitioner choices.

### Cited Findings
- "10 minutes a day is all you need"; stay at 90–100% for several consecutive days before increasing difficulty. — [Advancing Musician](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-training) [FETCHED, PRACTITIONER]
- 20 minutes per day in an example learner; do exercises "for a set amount of time" each day. Add one chord when accuracy is above 90% for three days in a row. Plateau checks: are you practising often enough, did you increase difficulty too quickly? Track results in a notebook or spreadsheet. No number of items per session is specified. — [Tonedear: how to practice](https://tonedear.com/ear-training/how-to-practice) [FETCHED]
- Chord Crush free tier: 5 puzzles and 1 Rush game per day; measurable targets such as +100 rating points in a week. — [Hooktheory: ear training tips](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/ear-training-tips/) [FETCHED, MARKETING]
- Forum learner: about 7 attempts a day for a month (about 200) on Beginner songs. — [Hooktheory forum](https://forum.hooktheory.com/t/ideas-for-developing-the-ability-to-hear-chord-progressions-by-ear/159) [FETCHED, anecdote]
- Degree order variants: tetrachord 1–4 then 5–8 (Advancing Musician); tonic vs dominant, then dominant vs submediant (Kelley); Kodály so–mi–la, then do–re, last fa–ti; the user's proposed "1-5-3, then 6, 4, 2, 7" is not stated by any source I read. — see sources above [FETCHED / SNIPPET]
- Berklee sequence steps are "often repeated for weeks"; the full sequence spans four semesters (ET 1–4), per the secondary blog. — [fifths-marketing](https://fifths-marketing.pages.dev/blog/singing-the-changes-berklee/) [FETCHED, secondary]
- Plateau signal from a forum report: high drill scores without real-music transfer. — [SourceForge listing / user reports](https://sourceforge.net/app/functional-ear-trainer/) [SNIPPET]
- Kelley's expectation: steady but not always quick progress; those who find associative hearing difficult should start with a rudimentary, narrow focus. — [Kelley](https://www.robertkelleyphd.com/home/eartrain.htm) [FETCHED]
- The "24 attempts to reach roughly 80% proficiency" claim is attributed by the forum poster to Andrew Fuller and he could not verify it. Treat it as **[FOLKLORE]**. — [Hooktheory forum](https://forum.hooktheory.com/t/ideas-for-developing-the-ability-to-hear-chord-progressions-by-ear/159)

### Inferences
- A defensible app default: 10–20 minute sessions, difficulty gated by about 90% accuracy over three days (Tonedear and Advancing Musician agree on this), one new option at a time, and a mix of new and previously failed items.
- The sources say "too fast an increase" is the first plateau suspect, then "not often enough", then "no transfer to real music". Fixes to build in: automatic easing after a miss streak, a "real song" drill in every session, and a progress log by item.
- Item counts per session are unsupported by sources; any number (for example 20–30 items) would be the product team's choice.

### Gaps
- No source specifies the degree order 1-5-3-6-4-2-7. The nearest documented orders are Kodály's and Kelley's.
- No peer-reviewed evidence on optimal session length or spacing for ear training.
- Spaced-repetition use in ear training was not covered here.

## 6. Transcription / play-by-ear as the capstone

### Takeaway
Teachers converge on one order for transcribing: key and tempo first, then bass line, then chords as Roman numerals, then melody, always checking by playing along. It works best on simple chord-based songs.

### Cited Findings
- Search-summarised consensus from several tutorial sites (blogs, not peer-reviewed): find the key, then the bass line (turn the bass up), derive chords from the downbeat bass note, convert to Roman numerals (which makes the song transposable), find the tonic chord and common patterns, check by playing along; for complex songs riff-level transcription is needed. The order of bass vs key varies between sources. — [Musical U: how to transcribe](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/transcribe-music-like-pro-rhythm-section/), [Musical U: the play-by-ear process](https://www.musical-u.com/modules/playing-by-ear/the-play-by-ear-process/), [Guitar Chalk](https://www.guitarchalk.com/harmonized-scales-and-transcribing-music/) [SNIPPET]
- Tonedear: transcribe on your instrument, melody first then chords, or the reverse; practise both. — [Tonedear](https://tonedear.com/ear-training/how-to-practice) [FETCHED]
- Hooktheory suggests identifying progressions in songs from many genres and checking against TheoryTab. — [Hooktheory: ear training tips](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/ear-training-tips/) [FETCHED]
- Beato's "Guitar Lesson: Playing by Ear" (per a search summary): start with simple progressions, listen for chord tones, sing scale tones and transition notes. — [Rosetta archive of the video](https://rosetta.to/u/rickbeato/guitar-lesson-playing-by-ear) [SNIPPET; I did not watch the video]
- Karpinski's second edition adds dictation based on real music. — [W. W. Norton](https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9780393614251-manual-for-ear-training-and-sight-singing-6bffa374-5844-4964-b0f8-61b5c82239ac/features) [FETCHED]

### Inferences
- The transcription capstone maps to a staged app flow: tonic (hum or tap), then bass degrees, then the chord function per bar, then the whole loop. This is the same ladder as sections 1, 2 and 4 in a real-song context. It also supports the "formula aloud / what is next" style of song exercises.
- The Roman-numeral output is the portable skill the sources value ("transposable"); this agrees with an app that answers only in degrees.

### Gaps
- No primary-source detail from Adam Neely, 12tone, David Bruce or Mary Spender; none was found for this topic in these searches.
- No evidence on how much transcription practice is needed before skills transfer.
- Video references (Beato, etc.) are only from search summaries; no precise timestamps were verified.
